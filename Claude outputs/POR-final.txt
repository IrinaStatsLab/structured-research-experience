Multi-view data — measurements collected on the same samples from multiple sources, or views — have become increasingly common with advances in multi-omics platforms and wearable sensing. The Cancer Genome Atlas, for example, characterizes the same subjects across several omics platforms; wearable studies similarly record glucose, activity, and heart rate on the same individuals. Each view offers only a partial characterization, yet traditional dimension reduction techniques such as principal component analysis treat each view in isolation. Discovering coordinated signals at a global level, and improving the downstream predictions that depend on them, requires methodology that integrates information across views while distinguishing structure shared among them from structure specific to each. The need for such tools is widely recognized, and is reflected in two of the National Science Foundation's Big Ideas: Understanding the Rules of Life, and Harnessing Data for 21st Century Science and Engineering.

This project addressed three obstacles to that integration. The first is the mismatch between the Gaussian assumptions underlying classical methods and the data that modern platforms actually produce: single-cell RNA sequencing, mass spectrometry proteomics, and 16S microbiome profiling yield heavily right-skewed measurements with substantial zero inflation, many of those zeros arising from limited sequencing depth rather than true absence, while mutation data are binary. This mismatch leads to failure to identify shared signal across views, sensitivity of estimated signals to outliers, and degraded performance in downstream analyses. The second is the inability of existing methods to separate shared and individual signals when views arrive as functions or distributions, as from wearable devices. The third is the absence of identifiability conditions and theoretical guarantees for the decompositions on which such methods rely.

INTELLECTUAL MERIT.

Addressing the first obstacle, the project developed a semiparametric framework based on latent and truncated Gaussian copula models that accommodates continuous, binary, and zero-inflated views within a single joint dimension reduction framework, with corresponding developments in classification, graphical modeling, and both deterministic and probabilistic principal component analysis. The resulting estimators were shown to attain the same rates of convergence as their Gaussian counterparts under substantially weaker distributional assumptions.

Addressing the second, the project developed methodology for functional and distributional responses, including a variable selection procedure for distributional regression whose computational cost was reduced by more than three orders of magnitude relative to existing implementations, rendering subsampling-based inference and biobank-scale application feasible.

Addressing the third, the project introduced a formulation of partially shared structure through hierarchical low-rank constraints with accompanying identifiability guarantees, and subsequently established explicit conditions on signal strength and noise, together with rates of convergence, under which joint and individual structures can be separated — resolving a question the literature had left open.

These methodological developments were pursued alongside scientific collaborations that tested them against substantive problems, including associations between pre-chemotherapy gut microbiome composition and treatment outcomes in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia, cortical surface imaging in autism, and the effect of obstructive sleep apnea on nocturnal glucose control. The award produced twenty peer-reviewed publications in statistics, machine learning, and domain science venues, among them Biometrika, the Journal of the American Statistical Association, Biometrics, the Annals of Applied Statistics, and the Journal of Machine Learning Research. One publication received the Journal of the American Statistical Association reproducibility award. All methodology was released as open-source R software.

BROADER IMPACTS.

The project's educational objectives were to improve students' preparation for conducting research, to strengthen their communication and programming skills, and to involve students from groups traditionally underrepresented in statistics. These objectives responded to a gap between coursework and research practice: undergraduate students typically enter research settings well prepared in theory and methods, yet without training in version control, reproducible computational workflows, the design of informative simulation studies, or the conventions of scientific writing and presentation.

The PI developed a structured research experience program treating these competencies as explicit instructional objectives. The program was formalized as a new upper-level undergraduate course, subsequently adopted by another instructor at the same institution, and its communication components were adapted for a master's-level biostatistics capstone. Research from the award further informed a revised graduate course in multivariate analysis and a new short course on digital health technologies, first offered at the 2026 Joint Statistical Meetings.

Fourteen undergraduate students participated directly in research supported by the award, and at least ten more were reached through the resulting coursework and co-mentored research experiences, with deliberate recruitment from groups underrepresented in statistics. Their work resulted in co-authorship on publications and software, poster and presentation awards at institutional and regional symposia, an honors thesis completed with distinction, and admission to doctoral programs. Eight doctoral students and two postdoctoral researchers contributed to the research, four of the doctoral students incorporating it into dissertation chapters. Instructional materials developed through the program, together with template repositories for organizing reproducible computational projects, have been publicly released under an open license.
