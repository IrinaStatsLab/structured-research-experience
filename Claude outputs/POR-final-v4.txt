Multi-view data — measurements collected on the same samples from several different sources, or views — have become increasingly common with advances in multi-omics platforms and wearable sensing. The Cancer Genome Atlas, for example, characterizes the same subjects across gene expression, mutation, and other omics platforms, while a wearable study may record glucose, physical activity, and heart rate on the same individuals. Each view captures only part of the picture. Many techniques exist for analyzing each view on its own, but discovering coordinated signals across views, and improving the predictions that depend on them, requires methods that combine the views while distinguishing what they share from what is specific to each. The need for such tools is widely recognized, and is reflected in two of the National Science Foundation's Big Ideas: Understanding the Rules of Life, and Harnessing Data for 21st Century Science and Engineering.

INTELLECTUAL MERIT.

This project developed new statistical and machine learning methods that take advantage of multi-view data, addressing three obstacles that had limited existing approaches.

The first is that classical methods assume the data are approximately Gaussian, while modern sequencing and proteomics technologies produce heavily skewed measurements with large numbers of zeros, and mutation data are binary. This mismatch causes methods to miss signal shared across views, to be unduly influenced by outliers, and to predict poorly. The project developed a flexible framework for dimension reduction, classification, and graphical modeling that accommodates continuous, binary, and zero-inflated views together, with mathematical guarantees matching those previously available only under the Gaussian assumption.

The second is that measurements from wearable devices arrive as curves or distributions rather than single numbers. The project developed methods for such data, including an algorithm more than a thousand times faster than previous implementations, making analyses of biobank-scale wearable data practical.

The third is that methods separating shared from view-specific structure had lacked guarantees that such a separation is well defined. The project established the conditions under which it is, and how accurately it can be recovered.

These developments were carried out with domain scientists, including studies of gut microbiome composition and treatment outcomes in pediatric leukemia, brain imaging in autism, and the effect of sleep apnea on overnight glucose control. The award produced twenty peer-reviewed publications in statistics, machine learning, and domain science journals, including Biometrika, the Journal of the American Statistical Association, Biometrics, and the Annals of Applied Statistics; one received the Journal of the American Statistical Association reproducibility award. All methods were released as open-source R software.

BROADER IMPACTS.

Training the next generation of quantitative researchers requires more than coursework. Undergraduate students typically enter research settings well prepared in statistical theory and methods, yet without training in version control, reproducible computational workflows, the design of informative simulation studies, or the conventions of scientific writing and presentation.

The PI developed a structured research experience program treating these as skills to be taught directly. The program became a new upper-level undergraduate course, was subsequently adopted by another instructor at the same institution, and its communication components were adapted for a master's-level biostatistics capstone. Research from the award further informed a revised graduate course in multivariate analysis and a new short course on digital health technologies, first offered at the 2026 Joint Statistical Meetings.

Fourteen undergraduate students participated directly in research supported by the award, and at least ten more were reached through the resulting coursework and co-mentored research experiences, with deliberate recruitment from groups underrepresented in statistics. Their work led to co-authorship on publications and software, poster and presentation awards, an honors thesis completed with distinction, and admission to doctoral programs. Eight doctoral students and two postdoctoral researchers contributed to the research, four of the doctoral students incorporating it into dissertation chapters. The instructional materials developed through the program, together with template repositories for organizing reproducible computational projects, have been publicly released under an open license.
