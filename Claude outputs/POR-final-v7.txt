Multi-view data, that is, measurements collected on the same samples from several different sources or views, have become increasingly common with advances in multi-omics platforms and wearable sensing. Large biomedical studies now routinely collect several such views: The Cancer Genome Atlas characterizes the same subjects across gene expression, mutation, and other omics platforms; sequencing studies profile microbiome composition alongside clinical measurements; and wearable studies record glucose, physical activity, and heart rate on the same individuals. Each view captures only part of the underlying signal, and the views seldom share a common structure: measurements may be continuous, binary, dominated by zeros, or be represented by time curves and distributions rather than single numbers. Many techniques exist for analyzing each view on its own, but discovering coordinated signals across views and leveraging data across different types and formats to improve predictions require new methods. The need for such tools is widely recognized and is reflected in two of the National Science Foundation's Big Ideas: Understanding the Rules of Life, and Harnessing Data for 21st Century Science and Engineering.

INTELLECTUAL MERIT.

This project developed new statistical and machine learning methods for multi-view data, addressing several obstacles that limited existing approaches. First, classical methods assume the data are approximately Gaussian, while modern sequencing and proteomics technologies produce heavily skewed measurements with many zero values, and mutation data are binary. This mismatch causes methods to miss signal shared across views, to be heavily influenced by outliers, and to predict poorly. The project developed a flexible framework for dimension reduction, classification, and graphical modeling that accommodates continuous, binary, and zero-inflated views together, with mathematical guarantees matching those previously available only under the Gaussian assumption. Second, measurements from wearable devices often arrive as curves or distributions, objects with special geometry. The project developed methods for such data, including automated processing software, dimension-reduction approaches, and fast computational algorithms that made previously unattainable analyses feasible. Third, methods separating shared from view-specific structure had lacked guarantees that such a separation is well defined. The project established the conditions under which it is, and how accurately it can be recovered.

These developments were carried out in collaboration with domain scientists and motivated by underlying scientific applications, including studies of gut microbiome composition and treatment outcomes in pediatric leukemia, brain imaging in autism, and the effect of sleep apnea on overnight glucose control. The award produced twenty peer-reviewed publications in statistics, machine learning, and domain science journals, one of which received the Journal of the American Statistical Association reproducibility award. All methods were released as open-source software, including multiple R packages supporting end-to-end workflows from raw data processing through analysis and visualization.

BROADER IMPACTS.

Training the next generation of quantitative researchers requires more than coursework. Undergraduate students typically enter research settings well prepared in statistical theory and methods, yet without training in version control, reproducible computational workflows, the design of informative simulation studies, or the conventions of scientific writing and presentation. The award supported the development of a structured research experience program to teach students these skills directly. This work produced a new undergraduate research course, a revised graduate course in advanced statistical learning, a revised graduate course in multivariate analysis, and educational materials covering scientific communication, the design and reporting of simulation studies, and reproducible research workflows, along with template repositories for organizing computational projects. These materials have been publicly released under a Creative Commons license for adoption and adaptation by other instructors and research groups.

During the award, the PI graduated five doctoral students and supervised two postdoctoral scholars, and supported four additional doctoral students through research projects and training. Fourteen undergraduate students participated directly in award-supported research, and at least ten more were reached through the resulting coursework and co-mentored research experiences. Their work led to co-authorship on publications and software, poster and presentation awards, an honors thesis completed with distinction, and admission to doctoral programs in STEM fields. The PI presented over thirty talks related to this award at conferences, departmental seminars, and workshops in the United States and internationally.
